    About and methods

    Snow-Wx collects open weather, snowpack and avalanche data for the mountains of the western US in one place. It is built by a meteorologist and is updated automatically every hour. Every number comes from a public source, listed below. It is not an official forecast: check the National Weather Service and your local avalanche center before heading out.

    NBM forecasts: "Expected" and probabilistic

    The ski-area and SNOTEL forecasts come from NOAA's National Blend of Models (NBM), a 2.5-km grid that blends dozens of weather models and ensembles and corrects them against observations. NBM produces two separate kinds of guidance:

    • Deterministic fields — one best estimate per hour. We call these "Expected": NBM's forecast amount, an average of possible outcomes. You typically have about a 1-in-3 chance of reaching it. The Powder Finder amounts, the totals and the accumulated-snow line are these values, added up over 6-hour steps, so they add up.
    • Probabilistic fields — percentiles (for example "25% chance of less than 2 inches, 75% chance of less than 9 inches") and exceedance probabilities ("40% chance of at least 6 inches"), built from the ensemble members.

    NOAA calibrates the two separately, so they can disagree. The expected amount is not the median and can even fall outside the likely range. This is expected; we show both as published rather than forcing one to fit the other. When they differ a lot, the forecast is uncertain.

    In practice NBM's deterministic snowfall behaves like an expected (average) amount: snowfall outcomes are skewed toward a few big totals, so the average sits above the middle outcome. In one full NBM run (8 Nov 2026) it was above NBM's own median in 99% of snowy 24- and 72-hour windows, and NBM's chance of getting at least that much was typically 30–40%. So "expected 4 in, 13–35% chance of at least 4 in" is consistent, not an error. (We used to call this amount "most likely"; that was misleading and was renamed "Expected" in November 2026.)

    Snowfall by day

    The area-page snowfall chart has one bar per day. Days run from 12 UTC to 12 UTC, about 4–5 AM to 4–5 AM in the West, because that is how NBM publishes its 24-hour snowfall percentiles.

    • The bar spans NBM's 25th to 75th percentile for that 24-hour window: the likely range, the middle half of NBM's possible outcomes. The label above it reads "low–high" in inches ("6–12″"; "<1″" when even the high end rounds to an inch or less). A day whose 75th percentile is under about ½ inch is greyed: little or no snow is likely.
    • The colour is the day's median (50th percentile) in the NWS snowfall colours.
    • No adding of percentiles. Percentiles of shorter windows cannot be added into percentiles of a longer one (the 75th percentile of a day is not the sum of two 12-hour 75th percentiles), and NBM publishes no 12-hour snowfall percentiles. That is why the chart shows whole days rather than day and night. A short first day (the hours from the run's start to the first 4–5 AM) has no NBM window of its own unless it is exactly 6 or 24 hours, so it shows NBM's expected amount instead, hatched grey.
    • NWS view: the NWS forecast has no ranges, so each day is one bar of the forecasters' amount (to 72 hours), in the same colours.

    Values are adjusted to the summit or base the same way as the expected amounts: NBM's 2.5-km grid values times the chance that the snow level is below that elevation.

    Accumulated snow

    The accumulated-snow chart adds up NBM's expected snowfall day by day, on the same 4–5 AM to 4–5 AM days as the daily chart, and labels each bar with the running total. It ends exactly where the "Next 10 days" total ends (a narrower last bar when that falls in the middle of a day), so its last bar matches that number. It shows no range: a range for a running total would need NBM percentiles for each total, and percentiles of separate days cannot be added up.

    Snowfall colours

    Every snowfall amount on the site (area-page bars and tiles, Powder Finder, My mountains, the forecast and observed snowfall layers on the map, and their legends) uses one colour table: the NWS snowfall colours as drawn on NOAA/NWS NOHRSC's National Snowfall Analysis maps. Classes (inches): trace, 0.1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 48, 60, 72, 96 and 120+; blues up to 6 inches, yellow to red from 6 to 36, purples above 48. Colour is never the only carrier: every bar, tile and swatch also shows its number. The model maps still use their own, older version of this table.

    Snowfall ranges and chances

    "Likely" ranges. Every range on the site (snowfall, snow level, snow ratio) shows likely X–Y: the middle half of NBM's outcomes, its 25th to 75th percentile. That is about a 1-in-4 chance of more than the high number and a 1-in-4 chance of less than the low number. (An earlier version showed the wider 10th–90th percentile range; the archive and the "chance of at least" fields still carry those, but the page text does not.) Where NBM has no single window for a span, only the expected amount is shown, never a range built by adding percentiles. In the NWS view only amounts are shown: the NWS forecast has no ranges.

    What is and is not added up. Percentiles are never summed across days or into 7- and 10-day totals. The 12-hour day/night ranges that are stored in the forecast files (not drawn on the site) add NBM's 6-hour windows rank by rank, a documented, conservative approximation that is slightly wide.

    Where the ranges come from. They are NBM's own snowfall percentiles. The 01, 07, 13 and 19 UTC NBM runs carry 6-, 24-, 48- and 72-hour snowfall percentiles out to about 11 days; we use those runs whenever they are available. The daily bars use the 24-hour windows only (see Snowfall by day). Powder Finder, My mountains and the area tiles show likely ranges for the next 24, 48 and 72 hours from NBM's own windows. Beyond 72 hours (7 and 10 days) they show the expected total only: a longer range would need percentiles added together, which is not valid.

    If only an older 00/06/12/18 UTC run is available, ranges after 48 hours are not in NBM's data. We then show a "derived range (fallback)": the 25th–75th percentiles of liquid precipitation × NBM's snow ratio × the chance of snow at that elevation. It is clearly labelled and narrower than the NBM range.

    Liquid ranges come from NBM's precipitation percentiles, which are published about 7½ hours after each run. They therefore usually come from an older run than the snowfall, and the page says which.

    "Chance of at least …" (Powder Finder and the area-page table) uses NBM's own exceedance probabilities for 24-, 48- and 72-hour windows ending at 12 UTC (about 4–5 AM in the West), at the thresholds NBM provides (from 2" up to 24" in 24 h, 2" to 36" in 48 h, 4" to 48" in 72 h; NBM publishes a 2" probability only for 24- and 48-hour windows, so none is shown for 72 hours and none is ever derived). We checked them against NBM's percentiles for the same windows across the western US: they agree within about 1–4 percentage points on average. The chances are for the 2.5-km grid cell and are not adjusted to the summit or base elevation. They are NBM-only, because the NWS forecast has no snowfall probabilities.

    Snow ratio and powder quality (a documented heuristic)

    The snow ratio is NBM's forecast snow-to-liquid ratio: how many inches of snow one inch of water makes. The area-page panel draws NBM's median (line) and the likely range (the middle half of its outcomes, blue shading) over the whole forecast from the same percentile series, solid and darker where precipitation is likely (at least 20% chance) and lighter and dotted elsewhere, like the snow-level chart. NBM gives a snow ratio in dry hours too (what snow would have, if any fell); where it gives none (a few warm, low places) nothing is drawn and the stretch is labelled "no snow forecast". The dashed lines mark powder quality, a simple, documented rule of thumb on the median ratio:

    • Dense: below 10:1 — heavy, wetter snow.
    • Medium: 10–15:1.
    • Blower: 15:1 and up — light, dry powder.

    A wind-affected marker (◆) is shown where NBM's summit gusts exceed 30 mph while it snows (wind can scour or pack snow). This is a heuristic from a forecast, not an accuracy claim, a measurement or an avalanche rating: real snow quality also depends on crystal type, settling, sun and the snow already on the ground. The thresholds may be tuned once we can verify them. Colours are chosen to be distinct from the snowfall colours; each band is also labelled in text.

    "Type" in Powder Finder and My mountains uses exactly the same three classes and thresholds (Dense below 10:1, Medium 10–15, Blower 15 and up), taken from the 12-hour periods with at least half an inch of snow. There is one definition for the whole site (the Finder, My mountains, the chart and the daily summary); it is a heuristic and its thresholds live in the site configuration.

    Daily Snow summary

    The sentence at the top of each forecast page is written by fixed rules, not by a person or an AI model. The rules and thresholds are in the open (config/summary_rules.yml in the project) and use only NBM numbers shown on the page: a deep-powder day needs NBM's own median (24 h) of at least 8 in, at least a 30% chance of 12 in or more, a snow ratio in the Blower class (15:1 or higher) and calm summit gusts; a storm cycle needs a 72-hour median of at least 18 in and quotes NBM's own 72-hour likely range (NBM's 25th–75th percentile); rain at the base, wind-affected snow and a dry stretch have their own rules. One headline wins by priority (alerts, rain at the base, powder day, storm cycle, dry, wind); the rest are bullets. Rules that use medians or chances only run when NBM publishes that window directly; otherwise the summary says so and uses the expected amount only. Powder quality and wind-affected are labelled heuristics, never accuracy claims or avalanche ratings. An active NWS winter alert for the place always comes first (it is read live, not stored in the summary). The summary also appears on Powder Finder rows (tag) and in My mountains. Every summary has a "Report a bad summary" link, and the owner reviews ten summaries against the NWS forecast discussion and observed totals each week.

    Precipitation by model and the agreement badge

    Each forecast page compares NBM with three NOAA models sampled at the summit: HRRR (to 48 h), RRFS (to 84 h) and GFS (to 10 days), plus the ECMWF operational run (to 10 days) when it is available. They are drawn as cumulative liquid-equivalent precipitation, because the models' own snowfall products differ. The "Snow (in)" toggle converts each model's liquid with NBM's median snow ratio and NBM's chance of snow at the point ("inches assume NBM SLR"); the NBM line is NBM's own expected snowfall. All lines start at the newest model start, so they are comparable; each model's own terrain height at the point is in the tooltip, since a coarse grid can sit well below or above the summit.

    Model agreement (High / Medium / Low) is a heuristic until verified, not an accuracy claim. Over the longest window (72, 48, 36, 24, 12 or 6 hours after the common start) that at least three of NBM, HRRR, RRFS and GFS reach, we take each model's liquid total and the median of those totals. A model "agrees" if it is within ±25% of the median (or within 0.10 inch when the median is small). If at least 80% of the sources agree the badge is High (all of three, all of four, 4 of 5), at least two thirds Medium, otherwise Low; when every model is under 0.20 inch (twice the 0.10 inch tolerance floor, so the models cannot disagree by more than that) there is nothing to disagree about, so no agreement rating is given: the badge reads Little precipitation expected (never "High"). The badge always uses liquid amounts, so the units toggle does not change it.

    Checked against real runs (Nov 18 2026, all 160 ski areas, NBM + HRRR + RRFS + GFS + ECMWF): with three sources the badge was High for 46% of areas, Medium for 38–39% and Low for 15–17%. When the ECMWF operational run became the fourth source the old "75% agree" rule made 78% of areas High (3 of 4 was enough), so the rule was tightened to 80% and two thirds: High 42%, Medium 36%, Low 23% on the same data, and unchanged for three sources. Models whose terrain sat more than 3,000 ft from the summit were outside the ±25% band no more often than models within 1,000 ft, so the height difference between a coarse grid and the summit does not by itself drive "Low". Because HRRR stops at 48 h, the 72-hour window is made of NBM, RRFS, GFS and ECMWF. The badge is still a heuristic that has not been verified against observations.

    Checked on days of different types (dev18, 00Z runs, 160 ski areas, NBM + RRFS + GFS + ECMWF over 72 hours): on two dry days (Nov 5 and 9) 80–81% of areas had every model under 0.20 inch and read "Little precipitation expected"; on two light days (Nov 13 and 15) 15–34% did; on the heavy-storm day (Nov 17) 18% did. Among the areas that were rated, High / Medium / Low were 6 / 38 / 56% (Nov 5), 13 / 42 / 45% (Nov 9), 19 / 48 / 33% (Nov 13), 17 / 38 / 45% (Nov 15) and 19 / 43 / 37% (Nov 17): no class passed 70% on any day type, so the 80% and two-thirds thresholds were left alone. The "little precipitation" limit was raised from 0.10 to 0.20 inch because the 0.10 inch tolerance means models under 0.20 inch cannot disagree by more than the tolerance allows: on the dry days every "High" rating was that kind of agreement about almost nothing. The badge remains a heuristic, not an accuracy claim.

    ECMWF. The comparison includes the ECMWF IFS operational high-resolution run (HRES), and a Models / Ensemble toggle switches to the ECMWF IFS ensemble (ENS): the perturbed members listed in ECMWF's open data (the page shows the actual count; at the time of writing the files carry 50 perturbed members and no control member, so the plume has 50, not 51). The single HRES run is drawn on the plume as a dashed, labelled reference line, not as a member. Each member is one complete scenario: we take its own running total, align it to the common start, and only then compute the 10, 25, 50, 75 and 90th percentiles and the mean across members at each time (linear interpolation between ordered members). Nothing is added across time, and none of NBM's percentile products are used. The plume is raw ECMWF guidance on a 0.25° grid (about 28 km): not bias-corrected, not elevation-adjusted and not the same product as NBM; the model's terrain height at the point is shown in the tooltip. Liquid is ECMWF's total precipitation (rain and melted snow); "Snow (in)" is ECMWF's snowfall water equivalent times NBM's median snow ratio ("inches assume NBM SLR"). The plume is greyed when the ECMWF run is more than 24 hours old, and "N of n members" counts members at or above an amount (n is the number of members in the file). If a forecast step could not be downloaded when the run was processed, that time is dropped for every member and the HRES line, drawn as a gap (never interpolated) and named under the chart; a run missing more than a tenth of its steps, or any of the first five days, is not published.

    Verification (in progress)

    We keep an archive of every NBM and NDFD snowfall forecast we publish (24-hour and 72-hour windows) and a script that compares the 24-hour forecasts with observed snow depth change at SNOTEL stations. Depth change is a lower bound of snowfall (settling and compaction remove depth), so a forecast that looks too high may simply be compared with a depth that settled. The comparison leaves out observations from the SNOTEL outage window, observations with fewer than 20 hourly reports in the day, stale observations and missing pairs, and it counts what it left out. With only days of archive it can show no seasonal skill, so we make no accuracy claim yet: the model-agreement badge and the powder-quality classes are documented heuristics until this comparison covers a season. Results will be published here.

    Storm total (observed)

    On each forecast page, under recent observations, you can pick a window (24 h, 48 h, 72 h or 7 days) and see what was observed: the change in snow depth and in snow water equivalent (SWE) at the nearest SNOTEL station, and for 24, 48 and 72 hours NOAA/NWS NOHRSC's National Snowfall Analysis at the summit and base. On the obs map the SNOTEL variable menu has the same 24-hour, 48-hour, 72-hour and 7-day depth changes, coloured with the NWS snowfall colours (losses brown). A depth change includes compaction and settling, so it is not snowfall; NOHRSC is an analysis on a grid, not a measurement at the lifts, and publishes no 7-day product. If the SNOTEL service is down, a saved copy older than the window shows "–" instead of an old value.

    NWS winter hazards layer and map defaults

    The obs map opens uncluttered: shaded relief, roads/towns/peaks, ski areas (at low zoom only the larger, hand-checked ones; the rest appear from zoom 8) and the NWS winter hazards. Avalanche danger, Sno-Parks, SNOTEL, airport stations (ASOS) and the snow overlays are one tap away in the layer menu (avalanche danger is also on every forecast page and ski-area popup); your choices are remembered in this browser, a link can carry them, and "Reset map layers to defaults" restores the opening view.

    The hazards layer draws the official NWS watch, warning and advisory areas straight from NOAA/NWS's public map service (mapservices.weather.noaa.gov), filtered to winter products: winter storm, winter weather, blizzard, ice storm, snow squall, lake-effect snow, extreme cold / cold weather, freezing fog, high wind and wind advisories, and avalanche watches, warnings and advisories. The list of products comes from the service's current product names. Outlines are heavy for warnings, dashed for watches and thin for advisories; tap an area to read the warning itself in the page: product, headline, issuing office, issued and expiry times (in your time zone), the area and the full description and instructions as NWS wrote them. "View on weather.gov" opens the issuing office's page. The text comes from the same alert feed as the alert banners; our update job also fetches the text of every alert the map draws, so your browser never contacts api.weather.gov (an alert whose text we could not get says so and links "View on weather.gov"). If the service does not respond (or answers with nothing while our own alert list has winter alerts) the map says so and lists the active winter alerts from our alert index instead of showing a blank. Always check weather.gov and your local avalanche center for the full, current information. Source: NOAA/NWS.

    Snow forecast map

    The obs map's overlay picker has a Forecast group next to the observed NOHRSC/SNODAS layers: NBM's expected snowfall for the next 24 hours, 72 hours and 7 days, and NBM's chance of at least 4, 6 or 12 inches in 24 and 72 hours. Windows run from about 5 AM (12Z) to 5 AM, starting with the first full window of the newest 01/07/13/19Z NBM run; the picker shows the window and the run. Values are NBM's own fields for its 2.5 km grid (not adjusted to a summit or base) and show the true grid cells when zoomed in.

    Snow level and chance of snow

    The snow level is the elevation where precipitation changes from rain to snow. NBM forecasts it with percentiles. From the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles we estimate the chance that the snow level is below the summit or base (NBM's snow-level percentiles run to about day 11 on the 01, 07, 13 and 19 UTC runs but only to 48 hours on the others; wherever the percentiles are missing, a ±800 ft ramp around NBM's deterministic snow level is used instead). Snowfall at the point is NBM's snowfall × that chance, so a base below the snow level gets less snow than the summit. The snow-level chart draws NBM's median snow level over the whole forecast, even when no snow is expected, with the likely range (the middle half of NBM's outcomes, its 25th–75th percentile) as the orange band; the line is solid where precipitation is likely (at least 20% chance) and dotted where it is unlikely. A grey, labelled stretch would mean NBM has no snow level there. Powder Finder shows the lowest snow level while precipitation is likely in the selected window, and "–" when little precipitation is expected in it.

    Elevation adjustment

    A 2.5-km grid cell has one elevation, usually between a ski area's base and summit. We take the NBM cell that contains each point and move the temperature to the point's elevation with a standard lapse rate of 6.5 °C per km (3.6 °F per 1000 ft; at most 4000 ft). Summit and base elevations come from the lowest and highest lift terminals (OpenSkiMap), checked against USGS 3DEP terrain. Wind and snowfall amounts are not lapse-adjusted; snowfall is scaled by the chance of snow as described above. The snow-to-liquid ratio is NBM's, limited to 4:1 to 25:1 (10:1 where NBM gives none).

    Sno-Parks and winter trailheads

    Each Sno-Park, snow play area and Nordic site has two forecast points: the trailhead (the parking area) and a clearly labelled "high terrain" point, the highest ground within 5 km (from USGS 3DEP), to show what snowmobilers and skiers may meet up high. Temperatures are adjusted to each elevation as for ski areas, and the temperature chart adds a wind chill line (NWS formula, using NBM's 10 m wind, so exposed terrain can feel colder). Snow depth at the trailhead comes from the SNODAS analysis. Avalanche danger is shown first: many Sno-Parks give access to avalanche terrain, and snowmobilers are among the most affected groups.

    NWS official forecast

    The "NWS official" toggle shows the National Digital Forecast Database (NDFD): the forecast written by NWS forecasters, on the same 2.5-km grid as NBM. It is shown raw, as issued, for the grid cell — not adjusted to the summit or base — because it already includes the forecasters' rain/snow decisions. Snowfall, precipitation and snow level are available to 72 hours.

    Forecaster discussion

    Area pages include the latest Area Forecast Discussion from the NWS office that forecasts for that area: the forecasters' own explanation of the weather pattern and their confidence. Paragraphs that mention the area or its region are highlighted.

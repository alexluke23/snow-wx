    About and methods

    Snow-Wx collects open weather, snowpack and avalanche data for the mountains of the western US in one place. It is built by a meteorologist and is updated automatically every hour. Every number comes from a public source, listed below. It is not an official forecast: check the National Weather Service and your local avalanche center before heading out.

    NBM forecasts: "Expected" and probabilistic

    The ski-area and SNOTEL forecasts come from NOAA's National Blend of Models (NBM), a 2.5-km grid that blends dozens of weather models and ensembles and corrects them against observations. NBM produces two separate kinds of guidance:

    • Deterministic fields — one best estimate per hour. We call these "Expected": NBM's forecast amount, an average of possible outcomes. You typically have about a 1-in-3 chance of reaching it. The Powder Finder amounts, the totals and the accumulated-snow line are these values, added up over 6-hour steps, so they add up.
    • Probabilistic fields — percentiles (for example "10% chance of less than 2 inches, 90% chance of less than 9 inches") and exceedance probabilities ("40% chance of at least 6 inches"), built from the ensemble members.

    NOAA calibrates the two separately, so they can disagree. The expected amount is not the median and can even fall outside the 10–90% range. This is expected; we show both as published rather than forcing one to fit the other. When they differ a lot, the forecast is uncertain.

    In practice NBM's deterministic snowfall behaves like an expected (average) amount: snowfall outcomes are skewed toward a few big totals, so the average sits above the middle outcome. In one full NBM run (8 Nov 2026) it was above NBM's own median in 99% of snowy 24- and 72-hour windows, and NBM's chance of getting at least that much was typically 30–40%. So "expected 4 in, 13–35% chance of at least 4 in" is consistent, not an error. (We used to call this amount "most likely"; that was misleading and was renamed "Expected" in November 2026.)

    Snowfall by day

    The area-page snowfall chart has one bar per day. Days run from 12 UTC to 12 UTC, about 4–5 AM to 4–5 AM in the West, because that is how NBM publishes its 24-hour snowfall percentiles.

    • The bar spans NBM's 25th to 75th percentile for that 24-hour window: the likely range, the middle half of NBM's possible outcomes. The label above it reads "low–high" in inches ("6–12″"; "<1″" when even the high end rounds to an inch or less). A day whose 75th percentile is under about ½ inch is greyed: little or no snow is likely.
    • The colour is the day's median (50th percentile) in the NWS snowfall colours.
    • No adding of percentiles. Percentiles of shorter windows cannot be added into percentiles of a longer one (the 75th percentile of a day is not the sum of two 12-hour 75th percentiles), and NBM publishes no 12-hour snowfall percentiles. That is why the chart shows whole days rather than day and night. A short first day (the hours from the run's start to the first 4–5 AM) has no NBM window of its own unless it is exactly 6 or 24 hours, so it shows NBM's expected amount instead, hatched grey.
    • NWS view: the NWS forecast has no ranges, so each day is one bar of the forecasters' amount (to 72 hours), in the same colours.

    Values are adjusted to the summit or base the same way as the expected amounts: NBM's 2.5-km grid values times the chance that the snow level is below that elevation.

    Accumulated snow

    The accumulated-snow chart adds up NBM's expected snowfall day by day, on the same 4–5 AM to 4–5 AM days as the daily chart, and labels each bar with the running total. It ends exactly where the "Next 10 days" total ends (a narrower last bar when that falls in the middle of a day), so its last bar matches that number. It shows no range: a range for a running total would need NBM percentiles for each total, and percentiles of separate days cannot be added up.

    Snowfall colours

    Every snowfall amount on the site (area-page bars and tiles, Powder Finder, My mountains, the forecast and observed snowfall layers on the map, and their legends) uses one colour table: the NWS snowfall colours as drawn on NOAA/NWS NOHRSC's National Snowfall Analysis maps. Classes (inches): trace, 0.1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 48, 60, 72, 96 and 120+; blues up to 6 inches, yellow to red from 6 to 36, purples above 48. Colour is never the only carrier: every bar, tile and swatch also shows its number. The model maps still use their own, older version of this table.

    Snowfall ranges and chances

    Ranges are NBM's own snowfall percentiles. The 01, 07, 13 and 19 UTC NBM runs carry 6-, 24-, 48- and 72-hour snowfall percentiles out to about 11 days; we use those runs whenever they are available. The daily bars use the 24-hour windows only (see Snowfall by day). Powder Finder, My mountains and the area tiles show 10–90% ranges for the next 24, 48 and 72 hours from NBM's own windows. Beyond 72 hours (7 and 10 days) they show the expected total only: a longer range would need percentiles added together, which is not valid.

    If only an older 00/06/12/18 UTC run is available, ranges after 48 hours are not in NBM's data. We then show a "derived range (fallback)": the 25th–75th percentiles of liquid precipitation × NBM's snow ratio × the chance of snow at that elevation. It is clearly labelled and narrower than the NBM range.

    Liquid ranges come from NBM's precipitation percentiles, which are published about 7½ hours after each run. They therefore usually come from an older run than the snowfall, and the page says which.

    "Chance of at least …" (Powder Finder and the area-page table) uses NBM's own exceedance probabilities for 24-, 48- and 72-hour windows ending at 12 UTC (about 4–5 AM in the West), at the thresholds NBM provides (from 2" up to 24" in 24 h, 2" to 36" in 48 h, 4" to 48" in 72 h; NBM publishes a 2" probability only for 24- and 48-hour windows, so none is shown for 72 hours and none is ever derived). We checked them against NBM's percentiles for the same windows across the western US: they agree within about 1–4 percentage points on average. The chances are for the 2.5-km grid cell and are not adjusted to the summit or base elevation. They are NBM-only, because the NWS forecast has no snowfall probabilities.

    Snow ratio and powder quality (a documented heuristic)

    The snow ratio is NBM's forecast snow-to-liquid ratio: how many inches of snow one inch of water makes. The area-page panel draws NBM's median (line) and 10–90% range (shading) only while snow is falling, because NBM's ratio is meaningless in dry hours. Behind it, powder quality is a simple, documented rule of thumb on the median ratio:

    • Dense: below 10:1 — heavy, wetter snow.
    • Medium: 10–15:1.
    • Blower: 15:1 and up — light, dry powder.

    A wind-affected marker (◆) is shown where NBM's summit gusts exceed 30 mph while it snows (wind can scour or pack snow). This is a heuristic from a forecast, not an accuracy claim, a measurement or an avalanche rating: real snow quality also depends on crystal type, settling, sun and the snow already on the ground. The thresholds may be tuned once we can verify them. Colours are chosen to be distinct from the snowfall colours; each band is also labelled in text.

    "Type" in Powder Finder and My mountains uses a finer four-step version of the same idea (Wet/heavy below 8:1, Average 8–12, Dry/light 12–18, Very dry/blower 18 and up, from the 12-hour periods with at least half an inch of snow) and is also a heuristic; its thresholds live in the site configuration.

    Snow forecast map

    The obs map's overlay picker has a Forecast group next to the observed NOHRSC/SNODAS layers: NBM's expected snowfall for the next 24 hours, 72 hours and 7 days, and NBM's chance of at least 4, 6 or 12 inches in 24 and 72 hours. Windows run from about 5 AM (12Z) to 5 AM, starting with the first full window of the newest 01/07/13/19Z NBM run; the picker shows the window and the run. Values are NBM's own fields for its 2.5 km grid (not adjusted to a summit or base) and show the true grid cells when zoomed in.

    Snow level and chance of snow

    The snow level is the elevation where precipitation changes from rain to snow. NBM forecasts it with percentiles. From the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles we estimate the chance that the snow level is below the summit or base (after 48 hours, a ±800 ft ramp around NBM's deterministic snow level is used). Snowfall at the point is NBM's snowfall × that chance, so a base below the snow level gets less snow than the summit. The snow-level chart is drawn solid where precipitation is likely (at least 20% chance) and dotted where it is unlikely. Powder Finder shows the lowest snow level while precipitation is likely in the selected window, and "–" when little precipitation is expected in it.

    Elevation adjustment

    A 2.5-km grid cell has one elevation, usually between a ski area's base and summit. We take the NBM cell that contains each point and move the temperature to the point's elevation with a standard lapse rate of 6.5 °C per km (3.6 °F per 1000 ft; at most 4000 ft). Summit and base elevations come from the lowest and highest lift terminals (OpenSkiMap), checked against USGS 3DEP terrain. Wind and snowfall amounts are not lapse-adjusted; snowfall is scaled by the chance of snow as described above.

    Sno-Parks and winter trailheads

    Each Sno-Park, snow play area and Nordic site has two forecast points: the trailhead (the parking area) and a clearly labelled "high terrain" point, the highest ground within 5 km (from USGS 3DEP), to show what snowmobilers and skiers may meet up high. Temperatures are adjusted to each elevation as for ski areas, and the temperature chart adds a wind chill line (NWS formula, using NBM's 10 m wind, so exposed terrain can feel colder). Snow depth at the trailhead comes from the SNODAS analysis. Avalanche danger is shown first: many Sno-Parks give access to avalanche terrain, and snowmobilers are among the most affected groups.

    NWS official forecast

    The "NWS official" toggle shows the National Digital Forecast Database (NDFD): the forecast written by NWS forecasters, on the same 2.5-km grid as NBM. It is shown raw, as issued, for the grid cell — not adjusted to the summit or base — because it already includes the forecasters' rain/snow decisions. Snowfall, precipitation and snow level are available to 72 hours.

    Forecaster discussion

    Area pages include the latest Area Forecast Discussion from the NWS office that forecasts for that area: the forecasters' own explanation of the weather pattern and their confidence. Paragraphs that mention the area or its region are highlighted.

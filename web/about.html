    About and methods

    Snow-Wx collects open weather, snowpack and avalanche data for the mountains of the western US in one place. It is built by a meteorologist and is updated automatically every hour. Every number comes from a public source, listed below. It is not an official forecast: check the National Weather Service and your local avalanche center before heading out.

    NBM forecasts: "Expected" and probabilistic

    The ski-area and SNOTEL forecasts come from NOAA's National Blend of Models (NBM), a 2.5-km grid that blends dozens of weather models and ensembles and corrects them against observations. NBM produces two separate kinds of guidance:

    • Deterministic fields — one best estimate per hour. We call these "Expected": NBM's forecast amount, an average of possible outcomes. You typically have about a 1-in-3 chance of reaching it; the 10–90% range shows what's typical. The bars on the area pages and the Powder Finder amounts are these values, added up over 12-hour day and night periods, so the bars add up to the totals.
    • Probabilistic fields — percentiles (for example "10% chance of less than 2 inches, 90% chance of less than 9 inches") and exceedance probabilities ("40% chance of at least 6 inches"), built from the ensemble members.

    NOAA calibrates the two separately, so they can disagree. The expected amount is not the median and can even fall outside the 10–90% range. This is expected; we show both as published rather than forcing one to fit the other. When they differ a lot, the forecast is uncertain.

    In practice NBM's deterministic snowfall behaves like an expected (average) amount: snowfall outcomes are skewed toward a few big totals, so the average sits above the middle outcome. In one full NBM run (8 Nov 2026) it was above NBM's own median in 99% of snowy 24- and 72-hour windows, and NBM's chance of getting at least that much was typically 30–40%. So "expected 4 in, 13–35% chance of at least 4 in" is consistent, not an error. (We used to call this amount "most likely"; that was misleading and was renamed "Expected" in November 2026.)

    Snowfall ranges and chances

    Ranges (whiskers and "NBM 10–90%") are NBM's own snowfall percentiles. The 01, 07, 13 and 19 UTC NBM runs carry 24-, 48- and 72-hour snowfall percentiles out to about 11 days; we use those runs whenever they are available. A range for a period is built from the 10th and 90th percentiles of the windows that cover it. Adding windows rank by rank (10th + 10th, 90th + 90th) makes the range slightly too wide, so treat it as an honest outer envelope.

    If only an older 00/06/12/18 UTC run is available, ranges after 48 hours are not in NBM's data. We then show a "derived range (fallback)": the 25th–75th percentiles of liquid precipitation × NBM's snow ratio × the chance of snow at that elevation. It is clearly labelled and narrower than the NBM range.

    Liquid ranges come from NBM's precipitation percentiles, which are published about 7½ hours after each run. They therefore usually come from an older run than the snowfall, and the page says which.

    "Chance of at least …" (Powder Finder and the area-page table) uses NBM's own exceedance probabilities for 24-, 48- and 72-hour windows ending at 12 UTC (about 4–5 AM in the West), at the thresholds NBM provides (up to 24" in 24 h, 36" in 48 h, 48" in 72 h). We checked them against NBM's percentiles for the same windows across the western US: they agree within about 1–4 percentage points on average. The chances are for the 2.5-km grid cell and are not adjusted to the summit or base elevation. They are NBM-only, because the NWS forecast has no snowfall probabilities.

    Snow forecast map

    The obs map's overlay picker has a Forecast group next to the observed NOHRSC/SNODAS layers: NBM's expected snowfall for the next 24 hours, 72 hours and 7 days, and NBM's chance of at least 4, 6 or 12 inches in 24 and 72 hours. Windows run from about 5 AM (12Z) to 5 AM, starting with the first full window of the newest 01/07/13/19Z NBM run; the picker shows the window and the run. Values are NBM's own fields for its 2.5 km grid (not adjusted to a summit or base) and show the true grid cells when zoomed in.

    Snow level and chance of snow

    The snow level is the elevation where precipitation changes from rain to snow. NBM forecasts it with percentiles. From the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles we estimate the chance that the snow level is below the summit or base (after 48 hours, a ±800 ft ramp around NBM's deterministic snow level is used). Snowfall at the point is NBM's snowfall × that chance, so a base below the snow level gets less snow than the summit. The snow-level chart is drawn solid where precipitation is likely (at least 20% chance) and dotted where it is unlikely. Powder Finder shows the lowest snow level while precipitation is likely in the selected window, and "–" when little precipitation is expected in it.

    Elevation adjustment

    A 2.5-km grid cell has one elevation, usually between a ski area's base and summit. We take the NBM cell that contains each point and move the temperature to the point's elevation with a standard lapse rate of 6.5 °C per km (3.6 °F per 1000 ft; at most 4000 ft). Summit and base elevations come from the lowest and highest lift terminals (OpenSkiMap), checked against USGS 3DEP terrain. Wind and snowfall amounts are not lapse-adjusted; snowfall is scaled by the chance of snow as described above.

    Sno-Parks and winter trailheads

    Each Sno-Park, snow play area and Nordic site has two forecast points: the trailhead (the parking area) and a clearly labelled "high terrain" point, the highest ground within 5 km (from USGS 3DEP), to show what snowmobilers and skiers may meet up high. Temperatures are adjusted to each elevation as for ski areas, and the temperature chart adds a wind chill line (NWS formula, using NBM's 10 m wind, so exposed terrain can feel colder). Snow depth at the trailhead comes from the SNODAS analysis. Avalanche danger is shown first: many Sno-Parks give access to avalanche terrain, and snowmobilers are among the most affected groups.

    NWS official forecast

    The "NWS official" toggle shows the National Digital Forecast Database (NDFD): the forecast written by NWS forecasters, on the same 2.5-km grid as NBM. It is shown raw, as issued, for the grid cell — not adjusted to the summit or base — because it already includes the forecasters' rain/snow decisions. Snowfall, precipitation and snow level are available to 72 hours.

    Ridge-top wind

    NBM's wind is a 10-metre wind for a 2.5-km grid cell, and that cell's elevation is usually well below an exposed summit or ridge. Winds near the ground are slowed by friction (trees, terrain, valleys), and winds increase with height in most storms, so NBM 10-m wind typically underestimates what you feel on an exposed ridge, often by a factor of two or more in strong flow.

    The "ridge wind" shown on area pages and in Powder Finder is the free-air wind at the summit's height: taken from the HRRR (0–48 h) and RRFS (to 84 h) models' pressure-level winds and interpolated to the pressure at the summit elevation. This is what a well-exposed ridge experiences; sheltered terrain and the lee side of ridges will see less, and gusts can be higher.

    Details: we use the model grid column nearest the summit and interpolate the wind in height between the pressure levels around the summit (RRFS every 50 mb; HRRR only 850, 700 and 500 mb, so between those its values are interpolated over 1.5–2.5 km of height). Levels that lie below the model's own terrain are skipped. HRRR is used to 48 hours, then RRFS to 84 hours.

    Forecaster discussion

    Area pages include the latest Area Forecast Discussion from the NWS office that forecasts for that area: the forecasters' own explanation of the weather pattern and their confidence. Paragraphs that mention the area or its region are highlighted.
